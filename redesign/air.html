Health guidance

What the colours ask of you

Every reading on this page is banded by the US EPA PM2.5 scale. Here is what each band means — and what to do when your street turns that colour.

Good

0 – 12 µg/m³

Air quality poses little or no health risk. Particulate levels are near the cleanest the WHO considers safe.

What to doEnjoy it. Walk, run, cycle — and open the windows while it lasts.

Moderate

12.1 – 35.4 µg/m³

Acceptable for most people, but already above the WHO guideline. Unusually sensitive individuals may notice effects.

What to doIf you're unusually sensitive to particle pollution, consider shortening intense outdoor exercise.

Unhealthy for sensitive groups

35.5 – 55.4 µg/m³

Children, older adults, pregnant people and anyone with heart or lung disease can start to feel effects.

What to doSensitive groups: reduce prolonged or heavy exertion outdoors. Everyone else: watch for symptoms.

Unhealthy

55.5 – 150.4 µg/m³

Everyone may begin to experience effects — irritated airways, coughing, shortness of breath. Sensitive groups face more serious ones.

What to doCut back outdoor exertion; move workouts indoors. Sensitive groups should avoid prolonged time outside.

Very unhealthy

150.5 – 250.4 µg/m³

Health alert: the risk of effects is increased for everyone, not just sensitive groups.

What to doAvoid prolonged exertion outdoors. Keep children and elders indoors; close windows, filter air if you can, mask up outside.

Hazardous

> 250.4 µg/m³

Emergency conditions. The entire population is likely to be affected; this is the air of severe dust storms and open burning.

What to doStay indoors with windows sealed. Avoid all outdoor activity. Follow local public-health alerts.

Bands follow the US EPA breakpoints for 24-hour PM2.5. The WHO guideline (10 µg/m³ annual mean) is stricter than all but the Good band.

Why air, first

The quiet emergency in every breath

Air pollution is linked to roughly one in nine deaths worldwide — a toll that makes it one of the greatest environmental risks to health. The World Health Organization attributes about seven million premature deaths every year to it: around 4.2 million from ambient (outdoor) pollution and 3.8 million from household air — the smoke of indoor stoves and open fires.

The particles that do the damage are the ones you cannot see. PM2.5 — fine particulate matter under 2.5 microns wide — slips past the body's defences into the bloodstream, driving strokes, heart disease, lung cancer and COPD. And across much of Africa, the street-level data needed to fight it barely exists. That is the gap this network was built to close: evidence, block by block, breath by breath.

Under the hood

A laser, a fan, and a Wi-Fi chip

Every air reading on this page comes from an SDS011 particulate sensor — the workhorse of the open-source Luftdaten citizen-sensing movement. A small fan draws air through a chamber where a laser beam scatters off airborne particles; from that scatter the sensor counts what's floating in the air and reports PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations in µg/m³ every few minutes.

The sensor rides on a low-cost microcontroller that pushes each reading over Wi-Fi — or GSM where connectivity is thin — to the open sensors.AFRICA platform, where it is mapped, archived and published. The whole station costs about as much as a pair of shoes, mounts on a window ledge, and is assembled by the citizens who host it. That is the point: cheap enough for a household, honest enough for a newsroom.
